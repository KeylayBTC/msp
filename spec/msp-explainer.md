# Multisig Session Protocol (MSP) — The Explainer

*Renamed 2026-10-07 from "Keylay Protocol." Keylay is the app this protocol grew out of; where this explainer says "your app" or "Keylay," it means that app. Where it describes the protocol, it says MSP.*

**What this is:** a companion to `msp-spec-v0.1-draft.md` that explains the *why* behind every decision in that document, written for someone who is a capable programmer but not a cryptographer or protocol engineer. Section numbers match the spec, so you can read them side by side. Where a decision came out of your own prior analysis (the May generalization doc, the June API-feasibility doc, or the August Blockchain Commons research brief), that's noted, so you can trace each choice back to its source.

**How to read it:** each section states the decision, explains the concepts needed to understand it, gives the reasoning, and — where it matters — names what was rejected and what would go wrong if the other choice had been made. Terms are defined the first time they appear.

## §1 — Why the protocol is shaped like this at all

**Decision: MSP defines session, messages, and transport — and refuses to define payload formats.**

A protocol earns adoption by being small. Wallets already speak BSMS (BIP-129, the Bitcoin Secure Multisig Setup standard — a file format for exchanging keys and the final wallet definition) and PSBT (BIP-174, the standard format for a bitcoin transaction that is passed around to collect signatures). If MSP redefined any of that, every wallet would have to implement MSP's version *next to* the standard version, and the honest answer to "why?" would be "no reason." So the protocol treats those formats as cargo. It only builds the truck: a private channel, labeled boxes, and delivery routes.

This is also the direct conclusion of your June feasibility analysis: the thing wallet teams will accept from an outside project is a spec that asks little. The **signer role** in §9 is the concrete expression of that — the smallest possible thing a wallet can implement and still participate.

**Decision: async, co-coordinators, and group encryption are explicitly out of scope for v0.1 — but written down anyway (Appendix A).**

There's a difference between "we haven't decided" and "we decided not yet." The first invites two implementers to fill the gap differently, and then you have two incompatible dialects — a fork of your spec before it's even frozen. The second reserves the space: message type numbers are set aside (§6.2), the design direction is recorded, and anyone extending the protocol extends it the same way. Your May doc flagged coordinator consensus as exactly this kind of fork risk. The fix costs a few paragraphs now.

**Why "MUST/SHOULD/MAY" language (§1.2):** specs are read by people trying to do the minimum. Requirement keywords make the minimum unambiguous. "Participants verify the descriptor" reads as advice; "MUST verify" reads as a conformance test you can fail.

## §2 — Terminology choices worth noticing

**"The initiator has no capability the other participants lack."** (Revised 2026-10-06; earlier drafts had a *coordinator* who assembled the descriptor and whom everyone then checked.) The sentence now carries more than it did. The initiator proposes the terms — m, n, script type, derivation template, network — and that is the whole of its special role. It does not collect the keys and hand back a descriptor, because nobody does: every participant sends its key to every other participant, and every participant builds the descriptor itself (§6.6a). The question "what if the coordinator cheats?" used to be answered with a list of checks. It is now answered with "cheat *how*?" — the initiator never holds anything the others don't, and never sends anything the others can't compute. What "reduce trust in the coordination layer" cashes out to: there is no coordinator in the trust model. A hostile initiator can propose bad terms, but proposing isn't deciding: every participant's screen shows the terms in plain form ("2-of-2 · P2WSH · mainnet") and nobody contributes a key until they accept them (§6.6). The display-and-accept step is what turns the terms from something you trust the initiator about into something you consented to yourself; the SAS (short authentication string, §6.8) has nothing to say about the terms — it establishes who is in the session, and it does that before the terms are even sent. That's why §4.3 no longer lists the initiator at all. What any participant, initiator included, can still do is refuse to continue: a stalled ceremony costs time, never a wrong wallet.

**How the protocol knows who the initiator is (2026-11-18).** It doesn't, until someone acts. Both participants send the same hello and the same nonce-only reveal, and compare the same string; nothing in that exchange says who is starting or what for. Then exactly one participant sends the first ceremony message — SESSION_INIT if this is a setup, PSBT_REQUEST if it is a signing session — and *that* is what makes it the initiator and names the kind of session. The users decided out of band (one tapped "start," the other "join"), and the protocol enforces the decision: if both send an opening message, or the wrong kind arrives (your app is waiting to co-sign and a SESSION_INIT shows up), the session aborts with `role_conflict`. The earlier draft left this to inference, and a reviewer pointed out that two implementations could reasonably wait for different messages after pairing. The reason the kind of session isn't in the hello or the reveal is deliberate: anyone who got in with a leaked code should learn nothing about what the session is for before the humans catch them.

**"Relay is trusted only for best-effort delivery."** The relay can drop everything and the worst outcome is a failed ceremony, never a compromised one. That property is what allows §7's transports to be swappable — a hostile Wi-Fi network, your hosted relay, and a public Nostr relay are all held to the same (low) standard.

## §3 — Why these cryptographic building blocks

A quick tour of the toolbox, because everything later uses these:

- **SHA-256** — a hash function: turns any input into a fixed 32-byte fingerprint. One-way (can't recover the input) and collision-resistant (can't find two inputs with the same output). The bitcoin ecosystem's default hash; no reason to use anything else.
- **PBKDF2** (Password-Based Key Derivation Function 2) — deliberately *slow* hashing. You use it on things a human chose or might guess, so that an attacker who wants to try millions of guesses pays the slow cost millions of times. 300,000 iterations matches what the app already ships.
- **X25519** — a Diffie-Hellman key exchange over Curve25519. Diffie-Hellman (DH) is the trick where two parties each generate a keypair, swap *public* keys, and each combine their own private key with the other's public key — both arrive at the same shared secret, while an eavesdropper who saw both public keys cannot compute it. X25519 is the modern, hard-to-misuse choice; it's what Signal uses.
- **HKDF** (HMAC-based Key Derivation Function) — the "key kitchen." Takes one secret plus labels and cooks as many *distinct* keys as you need. The labels (called *info* strings) guarantee a key made for one purpose is useless for another.
- **AES-256-GCM** — an AEAD cipher. AEAD (Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data) means it doesn't just scramble data, it also produces a tag proving nobody modified it, and it can additionally authenticate *unencrypted* bytes traveling alongside (that's the "associated data"). If one bit anywhere is tampered with, decryption fails loudly.
- **HMAC** — a keyed fingerprint: only someone holding the key can produce a valid one. Used before the session keys exist (§5.3).
- **Deterministic CBOR** — CBOR is a compact binary cousin of JSON. "Deterministic" means the rules pin down exactly one valid encoding of any message. Why that matters is in §6.3 below.

**Why AES-256-GCM and not ChaCha20-Poly1305?** Your May doc called ChaCha20-Poly1305 "the natural choice," and in the abstract it is — it's simpler to implement safely in software. But the reference implementation is a browser app, and browsers' built-in WebCrypto API implements AES-GCM natively and does **not** implement ChaCha20-Poly1305. Choosing ChaCha20 would force the reference implementation to ship its own cipher code in JavaScript — the exact kind of hand-rolled cryptography this project should never do. So: AES-256-GCM mandatory (and it's what the app already uses, audited in your April security review), ChaCha20 available later through the capability bits (§5.5) for platforms that prefer it. This is a case where "best primitive on paper" loses to "primitive the platform implements for you."

**Why PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA512?** Two decisions here. *PBKDF2 and not Argon2id:* same logic as the cipher choice. Argon2id is genuinely better against GPU-equipped attackers (it's *memory-hard* — cracking rigs can't parallelize it cheaply), and your security review already notes this. But WebCrypto has PBKDF2 built in and not Argon2id. Rather than pretend, the spec ships PBKDF2 and builds the `kdf` field (§5.2) so Argon2id can arrive later without a flag day — a coordinated cutover where every implementation must switch simultaneously, which is the most failure-prone event in protocol evolution. Also worth internalizing: KDF strength matters most for *weaker* codes. A generated random code (§5.1) starts at ~49.5 bits (the 10-character floor) and rises with length; the PBKDF2 stretch is what makes even the floor defensible offline (each guess costs 300,000 hashes). *SHA-512 inside PBKDF2 rather than SHA-256:* SHA-512 is somewhat slower per call on the GPUs attackers use (64-bit arithmetic, larger state) and no slower for a legitimate client doing it once per session, so it is a free increase in the attacker's cost. It is also what Keylay v0.9.0 shipped; the spec was changed on 2026-10-04 to match it, and the §10.1/§10.3 vectors were recomputed, and forward secrecy (§5.4) means a code cracked *after* the fact reveals no past traffic. The slow KDF is the seatbelt — most valuable for the user who types "wallet2026," and still working for the one who accepts a 10-character generated code.

## §4 — The threat model: deciding what you're fighting

Writing down what you *don't* defend against is the part most projects skip, and it's the part your May doc insisted on ("'reduces trust' without a stated residual is a marketing claim"). Three points deserve expansion:

**"Code knowledge equals membership" is a feature being honest about itself.** MSP has no accounts and no identities — that's its privacy story. The unavoidable flip side: the only credential is the code. The spec doesn't pretend otherwise; instead it makes the code strong by default (§5.1), single-use (§5.6), and — critically — adds a detector for the case where the code leaks anyway: the SAS check (§6.8). Notice the structure: *prevention* (entropy), *containment* (single-use), *detection* (SAS). Layered, and each layer stated. Since 2026-11-18 the detector also runs *first*: nothing but hellos and random nonces is sent before the humans have compared the string, so someone who gets in with a leaked code is caught before they have received an account key or a transaction. The earlier draft compared the SAS at the end of setup, after the keys had already been exchanged; an external review pointed out that abort could stop the funding but not take the keys back.

**What the spec promises each participant, separately (§4.2, revised 2026-11-18).** The old sentence was "a stalled or aborted ceremony produces no wallet and no signature." The second half isn't true: once your app has handed a signed PSBT to the network, no later abort can recall it. So the guarantees are now stated per participant — you never end up with a *wrong* wallet; you never release a signature you didn't approve; a released signature may be out there even if the session then died — and the app has to show "signature may have been sent" as a distinct state from "nothing was sent." Honest states beat comforting ones.

**Forward secrecy, defined once, carefully.** Forward secrecy (FS) means: if an attacker records your encrypted traffic today and steals your keys *next year*, the recording stays unreadable. MSP gets FS from ephemeral keys — keypairs generated for one session and *deleted* when no longer needed. The recorded traffic can only be decrypted with keys that no longer exist anywhere. Your app's security review confirmed this property in the implementation ("private keys are set to null immediately after session key derivation"); the spec makes the deletion a MUST (§5.4) because FS lives or dies on that deletion, not on the fancy math. This sentence from the BC research brief is the load-bearing one: **"forward secrecy comes from the deletion, not from the handshake."**

**The residual trust statement (§4.3) is a conformance requirement, not documentation hygiene.** Requiring implementations to publish it means no MSP wallet can honestly market "trustless coordination." That protects the protocol's credibility from its own future implementers.

## §5 — Layer 1: turning a shared code into a private channel

### §5.1 The session code

**Why 31 characters — and why only for *generated* codes?** The generator draws from a 31-character set that drops `0, 1, i, l, o` — the characters people misread over the phone or mistype from a screen — so a generated code can be read off one screen and retyped on another without ambiguity. Your app already uses this 31-character alphabet; the spec just writes it down and adds the display grouping. The important subtlety the spec now makes explicit: this restriction is a property of *generation*, not *acceptance*. A code you *type yourself* may use any letter or digit (`[a-z0-9]`, 36 characters), including the five look-alikes — because most memorable codes contain them (`wallet` has `l` and `o`, `bitcoin` has `i` and `o`), and rejecting them would defeat the memorable-code feature. The cost is that a hand-shared memorable code with an `l`/`1` or `o`/`0` in it can be mistranscribed into a different, non-connecting channel; the mitigation is to steer people toward generated codes, not to narrow the alphabet.

**Why 10 characters minimum?** Entropy math: each character carries log₂(31) ≈ 4.95 bits, so 10 characters ≈ 49.5 bits. "Bits of entropy" measures how many guesses an attacker needs — n bits means about 2ⁿ⁻¹ tries on average. On its own 49.5 bits is strong against online guessing but not the 79+ bits you'd want against a determined *offline* attacker — which is exactly why the code is never used raw. Every code-derived value, the channel identifier included, is computed through the stretched key `K_code` (§5.2), so each offline guess costs a full PBKDF2-300k evaluation, and forward secrecy (§5.4) means a code recovered *after* the session reveals no past traffic. Ten stretched characters is the deliberate compromise: the code is read aloud over phone calls and typed on phones, and usability is a security property here — a code too painful to share correctly gets shared *incorrectly* (pasted into group chats, reused, shortened). (Longer codes remain a SHOULD for the paranoid; 10 is the floor.)

**How strong is a code someone made up?** You can't tell from the string. "Length times alphabet" says `bitcoinmultisig2026` has about 98 bits, but an attacker trying words and years finds it almost at once — that formula is right only for random codes like the generator's. The honest method models the attacker: break the code into the pieces a guesser would try first (words, common passwords, dates, keyboard runs like `qwerty`, repeats, `1234`) and add up what each costs. That's what zxcvbn does, and it's what the spec now asks for. The other half is saying it in terms a person feels — "someone holding the relay's records could find this in about a day" — rather than "31 bits." And one design consequence worth knowing: these warnings are strict because today's join lets the relay test guesses offline at GPU speed. The split-code + CPace join under review would make every guess a visible online attempt, at which point a short memorable phrase is fine. Making memorable codes safe is one of the better arguments for that change.

**Why rejection sampling?** A subtle bug your own security review caught and fixed (finding F-07): 256 possible byte values don't divide evenly by 31, so naive `byte % 31` makes the first few characters slightly more likely. The bias is tiny, but "tiny bias in the security-critical secret" is exactly what a reviewer flags. Rejection sampling (throw away bytes ≥ 248, so what remains divides evenly) makes the generator *correct by construction* — no argument about whether the bias matters, because there is no bias. The spec bakes in the fixed version.

**Why is the code a secret and not an address?** Because everything descends from it. If it appeared in a URL or a log, the log becomes the attack. This is also why §5.2 makes sure the transport only ever sees a *derived* value.

**Why does the spec spell out *normalization* in painful detail (§5.1)?** Because getting it wrong fails in the most confusing way there is: a *silent spinner*. The code is the sole input to `K_code` and `channel_id`, so any difference in how two implementations clean up the user's keystrokes — uppercasing, which characters count as separators, whether a typed `O` is rejected or "helpfully" fixed — produces a different channel. The two people then sit in two different rendezvous points, each waiting for the other, and *neither sees an error*, because neither ever sees the other at all. There's no bad-MAC to log, no failed decryption — just two spinners. So the normalization steps are normative protocol, not UI polish. The rule is uniform: lowercase everything, then remove every character that isn't a lowercase letter or digit, then enforce the length floor. Stripping the *whole* complement of `[a-z0-9]` — rather than a hand-picked list of "separators" — is deliberate: it means every way an implementation might group a code for display (spaces, hyphens, the middle-dot `·` the app actually uses) round-trips automatically, and there's no separator an implementation could add later that would silently break interop. Look-alikes aren't rejected, because they're legitimate content in a typed code; a non-ASCII homoglyph like Cyrillic `а` simply isn't in `[a-z0-9]`, so it's dropped, and a code carrying one just fails to connect (no silent wrong-channel, no security consequence). Test vectors in §10 pin it down: five spellings of the same code — including the app's own `·`-grouped display — that must all derive one `K_code`, plus the homoglyph case that strips short and is rejected by the length floor. (An earlier draft of this section stripped only space and hyphen, rejected non-ASCII outright, and canonicalized to uppercase — which both rejected the `·` the app itself renders and would have thrown out memorable codes; the uniform lowercase-alphanumeric rule replaces it.)

### §5.2 Derived values

**Why not just hash the code to get the channel ID (like the app does today)?** Two upgrades, both from standard practice:

1. **The channel ID is derived *through* the stretched key.** In the app, `channel_id = SHA-256(code)`, which means someone who sees channel IDs on the relay can test code guesses at full hash speed. Deriving through PBKDF2 first means each guess costs the attacker 300,000 hash operations. Cheap insurance, especially for weaker user-chosen codes.
2. **Domain separation.** Every derivation gets its own label (`MSP-V1-KDF-SALT`, `MSP-V1-CHANNEL`, ...). This guarantees that no value computed for one purpose can ever be confused with, or substituted for, a value computed for another — even if the same inputs feed both. It's the cryptographic equivalent of never reusing a variable for two meanings. The `V1` in every label means a future protocol version produces entirely unrelated values, killing cross-version replay tricks at the root.

**Why a fixed public salt?** A salt normally randomizes hashing so attackers can't precompute tables. Here the salt must be *derivable by both sides from nothing but the code* — there's no channel yet to send a random salt over. A fixed, versioned salt is the standard resolution (it still separates MSP's derivations from every other PBKDF2 use in the world).

**Why the spec stopped promising "KDF agility" (2026-11-18).** Earlier text said the `kdf` byte in the hello would let a stronger function (Argon2id) arrive later "without a flag day." A reviewer pointed out the circularity: the channel ID is computed *through* the KDF, so two people stretching the same code with different functions land in two different rooms and never see each other's hello to negotiate anything. The byte is still there — it's where a future rule will live — but the spec now says plainly that v0.1 pins one KDF and that switching will need a bootstrap rule nobody has written yet. A reserved byte is not a migration plan.

### §5.3 The join handshake

**Why MAC the hello?** The hello announces "here is my public key for this session." Without authentication, a malicious relay could swap in *its* public key and sit in the middle — reading everything, re-encrypting to each side (a man-in-the-middle, MitM). The HMAC under `K_code` means only someone who knows the code can produce an acceptable hello. The relay doesn't know the code. This is the app's existing HMAC handshake, kept and specified precisely.

**Why ignore bad MACs *silently*?** If you respond with an error, a code-guesser learns two things: the channel exists, and their guess was wrong (so try the next one). Silence gives them nothing — they can't distinguish "wrong code" from "nobody home."

**Why is the hello a fixed 109-byte string (77 before 2026-11-09) instead of CBOR like everything else?** (Changed 2026-10-08.) Two reasons, both about safety at the very first message. First, the MAC has to be checkable *before* you run any parser on attacker-supplied bytes — the hello is the first thing a stranger sends you, and you don't want a parser bug reachable ahead of authentication. The earlier draft claimed this while encoding the hello as CBOR, which meant you had to decode the CBOR to find the MAC in the first place; the claim wasn't true. Now the hello *is* the fixed layout: the first 77 bytes (`"MSP-HELLO" || v || caps || kdf || pk || cm`) are exactly what the MAC covers, and the MAC is the last 32. Every field sits at a fixed offset, so there is nothing to parse, one valid form, no "did we both encode this the same way?" question, and no way to express a version or KDF number too big for its byte. The `MSP-HELLO` magic at the front is also how a receiver tells a hello from an envelope. It is the one label without a `V1` in it, on purpose: it never changes between versions, so an old implementation can still recognize a newer one's hello and say "wrong version" instead of ignoring it — and a label that never changes shouldn't claim a version. The version lives in the `v` byte right after it, which the MAC covers. Second, the failure mode of getting this wrong is the worst kind for adoption: because a bad-MAC hello is dropped *silently* (above), two implementations that serialize the MAC input differently see nothing but silence on the first message — no error, nowhere. So §5.3 gives the byte-by-byte layout and §10 publishes the exact 109-byte hello, its 77-byte MAC input, and the MAC, so an implementer whose MAC disagrees can see precisely which byte diverged. (There is no message-type field: the magic identifies a hello, and the MAC covers the magic.)

**Why does an unexpected key abort the session instead of being ignored? (revised 2026-11-18)** Your app learned the first half of this the hard way (review finding D-04): the original behavior — reset the session when a new key shows up — meant anyone holding the code could grief the session forever by spraying hellos with fresh keys, forcing endless resets. That's a denial-of-service (DoS) — an attack on availability rather than secrecy. The October fix was to *ignore* a new key "from the same transport peer." A reviewer then asked how a client is supposed to know which transport peer a hello came from, and the answer is that it can't: a client sees only its own connection to the relay, and the relay is the adversary. So the rule is now stated in the only terms a client can observe: there is one expected peer key; a valid hello from any other key means a third party holds the code, and the session aborts and says so. Yes, that means a code-holder can end a session by sending a hello. They could always do that; the design accepts a visible disruption over a silent one. Legitimate reconnection stays narrow (§8.3): same key, same hello, or start over.

### §5.4 Pairwise keys

**Why pairwise (a separate key per pair) and not one group key?** Your May doc already decided this and the spec keeps it: pairwise is simpler to implement correctly and simpler to audit. A group key raises questions pairwise doesn't have — who generates it, how it's distributed, what happens on membership change. For n ≤ 7 participants (real multisig sizes), the n² pair count is trivial. (Group encryption done *right* is MLS, and Appendix A.5 explains why that's deferred.)

**Why does `K_code` go into the HKDF salt?** This is a belt on top of the §5.3 suspenders. Even if an attacker somehow got its own hello accepted, the session key derivation itself mixes in the code — which it doesn't know. It ends up with keys that don't match anyone's. Two independent mechanisms have to fail before a MitM succeeds.

**Why do both public keys and the channel ID go into the `info`?** So the derived key is *bound* to exactly this pair in exactly this session. "Binding" means: change any ingredient and you get a completely different key. A recorded key exchange transplanted into another session, or another pair, produces garbage. Cross-context attacks die here.

**Why zero the private key immediately?** §4 covered it: that deletion *is* forward secrecy. The spec words it as a MUST with a defined moment ("immediately after every needed K_AB is derived") because "delete it eventually" is unenforceable and untestable.

### §5.5 Version negotiation

**Why solve this now, when there's exactly one implementation and zero adopters?** Because it cannot be added later. The first version that ships without a version field can never be told apart from anything, forever — every future release has to sniff and guess. Your May doc's open-questions list called this out ("must be defined before the first public spec release or it creates fragmentation immediately"), and it's correct. One byte in the hello now, or permanent archaeology later.

**Why a capability bitfield?** So optional features (ChaCha20, async, ratchet) can exist without splitting the protocol into dialects. Peers advertise; the intersection is what's used; unknown bits are ignored. It's how TLS, SSH, and every long-lived protocol survive evolution.

### §5.6 Code distribution

The protocol can't deliver its own bootstrap secret — someone has to hand the code across somehow, and that hop is genuinely outside the cryptography. What the spec *can* do: state the risk plainly (a code intercepted before join = full MitM exposure), give SHOULD-level guidance (different channel than the session; E2E-encrypted or in person), make codes single-use (a leaked code is worthless after the session), and point at the SAS check as the detector of exactly this failure. Your May doc identified code distribution as "part of the security surface"; this is that surface, handled as honestly as it can be.

## §6 — Layer 2: envelopes and the ceremony

### §6.1 The envelope

**Why integer map keys instead of named JSON fields?** Size. A QR code holds a few kilobytes at usable density; every byte of `"session_id":` overhead is space stolen from the payload. Integer-keyed CBOR headers cost 2 bytes per field.

**Why is the header authenticated but not encrypted?** The relay needs *some* routing information (which session, which recipient) to do its one job. Everything it sees is already meaningless outside the session (key IDs are hashes of ephemeral keys; the session ID is derived). Authenticating the header via AAD (§6.4) means the relay can *read* those routing fields but any *modification* breaks decryption. Read-only for the relay, tamper-proof for you.

**Why `mid`/`re` request-response pairing?** Three problems solved by a 16-byte message identity: duplicates are detectable (same `mid` twice = drop), responses are attributable (this PSBT_RESPONSE answers *that* PSBT_REQUEST, not some earlier one), and application-level replay is closed (a response can't be re-presented against a different request). This is deliberately the same discipline as Blockchain Commons' ARID (Apparently Random IDentifier) in BCR-2024-004 — adopted, not reinvented, per the research brief's verdict A: *"adopt the R/R message layer; do not invent one."* Where the brief's advice couldn't be followed literally (full Gordian Envelope adoption — see §6.10 below), the spec follows its *shape*: pairing IDs, and the next item.

**Why is `mid` deterministic rather than random (2026-11-05)?** Draft.1 used 16 random bytes, which made a *resent* message indistinguishable from a *new* one. On a live two-party relay that is harmless. On a store-and-forward transport that delivers at least once, or under any future threshold-signature round, a sender that re-issues the same logical message after a reconnect would hand the receiver two different identities for one event — and for nonce-bearing rounds, processing both is a key-leaking nonce reuse. Turon's Delving Bitcoin analysis names this as the first transport invariant: identity bound to (session, round, signer). The spec now derives `mid` from the channel, the sender's key, the message type, and a per-(sender, type) round number that both sides compute from ceremony state, so a resend carries the same `mid` and is ignored, and two different bodies under one `mid` are a sender contradiction and abort the session (`identity_conflict`). Replay protection by `ctr` is unchanged; it catches exact duplicates, and identity catches re-encrypted resends. Random identity was chosen first because the two-party relay never needed more; the asynchronous target (§1.1) is why it changed.

**Why the `note` field?** The Blockchain Commons research surfaced a finding that should reorder your priorities: when they published their classic multisig setup guide, users told them it was *too complex to use even with substantial money at stake*. The adversary is process fatigue, not cryptanalysis. Their Request/Response redesign attacked it by making every machine request carry a human explanation — and their measured result was user actions dropping from 31 to 14 and decision points from 5 to 2. The `note` field imports that lesson at a cost of one optional string.

### §6.2 The type registry

**Why numeric types with reserved ranges?** So extension is orderly. The multiple-initiator range (64–127) is the concrete expression of "out of scope *but reserved*" from §1. The private range (128–255) gives experimenters a sandbox that can never collide with the standard. The differing unknown-type rules (error on unknown core type, ignore unknown private type) mean a conforming v1 implementation behaves predictably when it meets a v1.5 peer.

### §6.3 Deterministic encoding

**The problem this solves:** most encodings allow the same logical message to be written as several different byte strings (JSON: key order, whitespace, number formats; even CBOR normally allows length-encoding choices). Now try to sign or MAC a message: the signature covers *bytes*. If sender and receiver serialize the "same" message differently, verification fails randomly — or worse, an attacker finds a second encoding that means something subtly different. Deterministic CBOR (RFC 8949 §4.2) eliminates the whole category: one message, one encoding, everywhere.

**Why this beats JSON here** (your May doc left this open as "needs to be decided"): (a) QR and Nostr are size-constrained; CBOR is 2–4× smaller; (b) signatures and MACs need byte-stable encoding, which JSON fundamentally lacks; (c) the surrounding ecosystem — UR, Gordian Envelope, dCBOR — is CBOR-native, which makes the §6.10 compatibility path a mapping instead of a translation layer. The cost is honesty about tooling: JSON can be debugged with your eyes, CBOR needs a decoder. That's a real cost and worth paying.

### §6.4 Encryption details

**Why deterministic counter-based nonces instead of random ones?** A nonce ("number used once") must never repeat under the same key — for GCM specifically, a repeat is catastrophic (it leaks the authentication key; this is the single most famous way to destroy GCM). Random 12-byte nonces are *probably* fine but carry birthday-collision risk if a session sends enormous message counts, and "probably fine" is a bad property to build on. A counter is *provably* fine: the (key, direction, counter) triple physically cannot repeat, because the counter only goes up, the key is unique per pair per session, and a single direction byte (who's the lexicographically smaller key — an arbitrary but unambiguous tiebreak) separates the two directions that share a pairwise key. One subtlety worth knowing about because the spec had to fix it in draft: putting a *truncated sender ID* in the nonce instead of a direction byte looks equivalent but isn't — two IDs can collide once truncated, and a collision means nonce reuse under the same key, the one unforgivable sin in GCM. The direction byte can't collide by construction. This is what "correct by construction" buys over "correct with overwhelming probability." Bonus: the nonce needs no transmission (the receiver reconstructs it from header fields), saving 12 bytes per message. This deliberately replaces the app's random-IV approach — noted in spec §14 so nobody assumes the app already conforms.

**Why the whole header as AAD?** Whatever isn't authenticated, an attacker will eventually modify. Binding all eight header fields means an envelope can't be re-typed (turn a DESCRIPTOR_CONFIRM into an ABORT), re-addressed, re-numbered, or re-paired without decryption failing. The rule of thumb the spec follows: encrypt what's secret, authenticate *everything*.

### §6.5 Replay protection

**Why "advance the counter only after successful decryption"?** Because your app had the opposite bug (finding F-01) and the failure mode is instructive: if you advance on receipt, a relay can inject garbage with counter 999, the client burns slot 999, and the *legitimate* message 999 arriving next is rejected as a replay. The attacker spent one forged packet to knock an honest message out of the sequence. Order of operations is the whole fix, and it costs nothing — which is why it's specified as a MUST rather than left as implementation wisdom.

**Why check `ctr` before attempting decryption?** Cheap DoS resistance: discarding an obvious replay costs a comparison; attempting decryption costs an AEAD operation. Make the attacker's cheapest move also your cheapest response.

### §6.6 The setup ceremony

**Why does KEY_CONTRIBUTION carry just a key expression, with no signature?** (Revised 2026-10-08.) Earlier drafts carried a full BSMS Round-1 record because it includes a signature by the contributed key, which looked like a neat answer to "how does an air-gapped device vouch for its key?" Then testing showed common wallets don't export Round-1 records at all — Nunchuk and BlueWallet only give out a key during a multisig setup they run themselves. So the protocol now takes what devices actually give you, `[fingerprint/path]xpub`, and gets the vouching from somewhere better: the device's own screen. Register the finished wallet on your hardware wallet and it tells you whether its key is in there; that catches a bridge or app that swapped your key, which a signature carried by that same app never fully could. The one thing a signature added — proof the contributor can actually sign — is better proven by a small test spend that every cosigner signs before real money goes in. Keeping the signature as optional was considered and rejected: optional to send means mandatory to verify, so every implementer would carry a signed-message verifier for a benefit the test spend gives anyway.

**Why is setup symmetric — everyone sends to everyone, everyone assembles?** (Revised 2026-10-06.) Because the alternative — one party assembles and distributes — is the Benma attack's front door, and the draft.1 design walked in and then posted guards. With pairwise encryption a distributing coordinator can hand Alice one descriptor and Bob another, each containing that person's real key and otherwise the attacker's; each checks "is my key there?" and passes. Draft.1's Appendix A.6 patched this with broadcast signed acknowledgements and an n-signatures rule — correct, and the most complicated construction in the document. The author's question that retired it: *can't every participant just assemble the descriptor themselves?* Yes. If KEY_CONTRIBUTION goes to everyone, each participant holds every cosigner's key, received directly from that cosigner over their own authenticated channel. Nobody sends a descriptor, so nobody can send two. The attack is not detected; it is not constructible. What remains is a short confirmation round (everyone broadcasts the hash of what they built; all must match) that catches a *signer* sending different records to different peers — which can't make that signer money, but would leave two people with different wallets — and catches two implementations whose assembly differs by a byte. The price is n(n−1) small messages instead of n−1, and one genuinely new piece of spec: §6.6a, which pins the assembly to the byte.

**Why no slot assignment at all?** (Replaces the FCFS discussion.) The May doc posed two options. Tokens issued by the coordinator before the session require... a secure pre-session channel to each participant, which is the exact thing the protocol exists to create. Circular dependency; dead on arrival. Draft.1 chose first-come-first-served by the coordinator. With symmetric assembly there is no coordinator to observe an order, so the order has to be a function of the keys: §6.6a sorts the key expressions byte-wise (which, since each begins with its fingerprint, means by fingerprint). In `sortedmulti` descriptors key order doesn't affect addresses anyway; it affects only the descriptor *string*, and the string has to be identical on every device for the hashes to match. Unsorted `multi` is out of scope for v0.1 — the day someone needs an ordered policy is the day slots come back, as an extension.

**Why "two-key wallets," and why that is a scope decision rather than a limitation hidden in a footnote (2026-11-18).** A v0.1 session has two participants and each contributes one key, so setup produces a wallet with two keys — threshold 1 or 2, and the consent screen says which (a 1-of-2 means either key alone can spend, and the screen must say so in words). The 2-of-3 example in the test vectors is a test of the *assembly function*, not something this version can run live. Three ways to reach a three-key wallet were on the table in November: raise the relay to three connections (the planned group session), chain pairwise sessions and forward keys between them (proposed by the external reviewer, then withdrawn by agreement), or let one client contribute two keys it holds (the D-15 memo option). The author chose to ship none of them in v0.1 and to say so in §1.3, rather than let readers infer a capability from a vector. Signing is broader than setup: any wallet both parties hold, however it was made, can be signed for; a 2-of-3 made elsewhere is signed by two of its holders in one session. The forwarding analysis is preserved in `notes/forwarding-and-multikey-note-2026-11-18.md` so the group-session design starts from it.

**Why duplicate keys are rejected by the 33-byte public key, not by the string (2026-11-18).** Two contributions can describe the same signing key under two labels: a different fingerprint, a different path, a different parent fingerprint in the serialization, even a different SLIP-132 prefix — and derive the same child keys. Without a check, both participants would happily confirm a "2-of-2" that one key spends from. Comparing the whole serialized xpub isn't enough, because BIP-32 serialization carries metadata (the parent fingerprint, the depth) that can differ while the key is identical. So the rule compares the decoded compressed public key — the thing that actually signs. Distinct keys still don't prove distinct people hold them; that stays in the residual trust statement.

**Why does DESCRIPTOR_CONFIRM carry both the hash and the first address?** The hash is the byte-exact check: two devices that assembled the same template produce the same 32 bytes, full stop. The first address is the semantic check and the human one — it digests every key in the descriptor through actual derivation, it is what BSMS itself asks participants to confirm, and it is the value a user can read off a hardware wallet's screen and compare. Keeping both means a mismatch tells you *which* layer disagreed: same address but different hash is a formatting divergence between implementations (a §6.6a bug); different addresses means somebody holds a different key set. (Again: adopt the payload standard's wisdom, give it transport.)

**Why is FINALIZE only an announcement?** Because a participant's reason to trust the wallet is already in its own hands — its own assembly, the matching CONFIRMs it collected, the SAS it compared at the start, the address it read off its device. A message from the initiator saying "we're done" adds no evidence. Draft.1 gave FINALIZE authority because the coordinator had done the assembling; take the assembling away and the authority goes with it.

**Why is `wallet_sig` optional?** It's valuable — a portable, offline-verifiable receipt that this participant verified this exact wallet (your May doc's open question about signed ACKs, answered "yes, optional"). But many hardware signers physically can't produce an arbitrary signature over a descriptor hash. If the field were mandatory, those devices couldn't conform, and the spec would have traded real adoption for a nice-to-have artifact. Optional with defined semantics: those who can, do; absence fails nothing.

**Why is there a named "pre-funding checkpoint" (2026-11-18)?** Because the spec had the right rule in the wrong place. §6.6a said, since October, that the first address you compare must come from your hardware wallet's screen, not from the browser — the browser is the thing that could be lying. But the live procedure in §6.8 only told people to compare the participant count and the SAS, and DESCRIPTOR_CONFIRM had already gone out from the online software. A reviewer followed the steps as written and showed that a user could finish the whole displayed ceremony without anyone ever asking a device for its address. So the step now has a name, a place (after the CONFIRMs match, before "ready to fund"), and three recorded outcomes: *device-verified*, *software-only* (your device can't show a multisig address, so you're protected against the other person's software but not your own, and the app has to say so), and *not done*. Two human steps in a setup, both now written down: the SAS at the start answers "who is here," the address before funding answers "which wallet did we build." BSMS has always asked for the address comparison; MSP now says when.

**Why can't FINALIZE come before the checks?** Sequencing as enforcement. FINALIZE is the "you may now import this wallet" signal; the SAS check and the pre-funding checkpoint are the detectors. Allowing the signal before the detectors run invites every implementation to make the checks skippable, and users skip what's skippable — the process-fatigue lesson again, applied defensively. Under the November order the SAS is confirmed before FINALIZE could even exist, so its `sas_confirmed` field is now always true; it stays in the body so the wire format doesn't churn.

### §6.7 Signing-phase validation

**The rule: no signer signs a PSBT unless it spends from a wallet that signer holds — and, since every signer makes that check against the same hash the requester sent, a session can only sign for a wallet the requester and the signer hold in common.** Your May doc identified the attack this kills, and it deserves restating because it's the strongest single line in the protocol: without this check, the entire setup ceremony provides *false* assurance. A coordinator (or someone who stole a session code for a fresh session) presents a PSBT spending from a *different* wallet — one the attacker controls — and a signer that signs "because the channel is authenticated" just authorized a theft. The channel proves *who is talking*, never *what is true*. Binding every signature request to `descriptor_hash`, and requiring input-by-input derivation checks against it, is what converts "encrypted session" into "safe ceremony." This is a MUST with teeth: an implementation that skips it is nonconforming, full stop. (The wording changed on 2026-11-18: "the descriptor that signer finalized" read as if the wallet had to come from an MSP setup, and "a wallet it already holds" read as if one signer's holding sufficed. Neither was meant. A wallet made anywhere qualifies, as long as it reconstructs to one of the supported templates — §6.6a now says exactly how — and the hash check is mutual by construction.)

**Why the request now carries the chain too (2026-11-18).** The canonical template encodes the network only through the key prefix, and testnet and signet share `tpub`, so two wallets with the same keys on those two chains have the *same* hash. Harmless for setup — you'd notice — but a signing request binds to a hash, so the request now says `network` as well, and the signer checks both against the wallet its user selected. Signet gets its own value (`2`) for the same reason.

**Why the spec now says what the user must approve, not only what the software must check (2026-11-18).** Checks (a)–(c) establish that the PSBT is a well-formed spend from the right wallet. They do not establish that you want to make it. A reviewer pointed out two gaps. First, the draft required external outputs to be *displayed* but never required the user to *approve* destinations, amounts, and fee — and a display nobody has to act on is a display people don't read. Second, and sharper: a PSBT can ask for a signature-hash type under which the signature doesn't commit to all the outputs (the `SIGHASH` modes let a signer sign "just my input" or "all inputs but only one output"). A signer could display every output faithfully and still produce a signature that lets someone change those outputs afterward. So v0.1 signers sign `SIGHASH_ALL` only — the mode where the signature covers every input and every output — and refuse anything else; and the approval screen (on the hardware wallet's own display where there is one) must show every destination in full, the amounts, the fee, and get an explicit yes. The fee requires knowing the input amounts, so a PSBT without witness UTXOs is refused. On the way back, the requester checks that the unsigned transaction it gets is the one it sent, that every signature already there survived, and that at least one *new* signature from a wallet key was added per input, before combining. And "success" has two meanings the app must keep apart: *signatures collected* (a 2-of-3 signed by one cosigner so far, carried to the next session) and *finalized* (threshold met, transaction complete, id computed locally). The fee needs every input's amount, not just the ones you're signing — a transaction mixing wallet inputs with someone else's can't show you a fee otherwise, and a fee you can't verify is a fee you refuse. None of this is MSP's invention — it's BIP-174's signer checklist and what good hardware wallets already do — but a protocol that says "the signer validates" without saying what, in a document whose whole pitch is that the setup ceremony makes signing safe, was making a promise it hadn't written down.

**Why the change output is checked too (added 2026-11-08).** The input check stops a signer being used for a wallet that is not its own. The check that protects the signer's *money* is the output check: a PSBT that does spend from your 2-of-3, pays a small amount to the stated recipient, and sends the change to an address the attacker controls. A signer that cannot recognize the change as its own wallet's shows the user a large payment to an unfamiliar address, and a user expecting a small spend may approve it. In a multisig the change address depends on every cosigner's key and the script policy, so only a signer holding the full descriptor can recompute it; that is why hardware wallets insist on registering a multisig before signing. The check is not MSP's invention and does not depend on the transport. What MSP adds is the setup integrity that makes it meaningful, because a change check against a poisoned descriptor passes for the attacker, and the rule that the bridge must not strip the derivation fields the device needs.

### §6.8 The SAS check

**What the check is, as of November 2026.** Right after the two hellos are exchanged, each app sends one small encrypted message revealing a random nonce it committed to in its hello. Each app checks the other's nonce against the commitment, computes a 12-character string from both keys and both nonces, and shows it with the participant count. The two people compare the string over a call or a verified message thread. If it matches, the session is authenticated and the ceremony begins — terms, keys, confirmations, or a signing request. If it doesn't, abort, and nothing of value has been sent. That is the whole of the first human step. It is the same in a setup and in a signing session, and it comes before anything else in both.

**Why the hello carries a commitment, and why the descriptor left the SAS (2026-11-09).** The comparison code exists to catch someone sitting in the middle swapping keys. It only works if that someone has to choose its values *before* seeing yours; otherwise it sees both honest sides first and searches for two substitutes whose codes collide, which for a 60-bit code is a birthday search of about 2^30 tries per side, minutes on a graphics card. The textbook fix is a commitment round before the keys are revealed, and that costs every session a round trip. The spec takes the other route the literature offers (Vaudenay's SAS protocols, Laur and Pasini's group version, Bluetooth's numeric comparison): commit to a random nonce, not to the key. Each hello carries a hash of a fresh nonce, sealed under the code MAC. The nonce is revealed later, inside an encrypted message, and only after the participant holds the other's hello. The comparison code is then a hash over both keys *and* both nonces. From the attacker's side, every value it can choose toward you is pinned in a hello you hold before you reveal your nonce, and your nonce is the last thing into the hash, so the attacker's chance of making both honest strings agree is about one in 2^59 per session, with no search that helps. The hello grows from 77 to 109 bytes.

**Why the reveal moved to its own message, and why setup now costs one more message (2026-11-18).** The November 9 design put the nonce inside KEY_CONTRIBUTION for setup sessions, so that setup needed no new message; signing sessions got a small SAS_REVEAL. An external reviewer found the cost of that economy: in a setup, the nonce arrived *together with the account key*, and the humans compared the string only after keys, assembly, and confirmations. If someone had gotten in with a leaked code, the mismatch would still stop the funding — but the xpubs were already in the attacker's hands, and an abort can't take them back. The November 7 study had weighed the SAS as a defense against key substitution and never weighed what the attacker *learns* before being caught. The author accepted the trade: every session, both kinds, now has a SAS_REVEAL from each side as its first encrypted message, and nothing else moves until both users have confirmed. One extra concurrent one-way message per setup session, in exchange for a single, early, teachable checkpoint — and for the sentence in Appendix A.6 ("the live protocol detects an interposer before any ceremony content is exchanged") becoming true instead of false. The security argument doesn't change shape: your rule is still "hold the other hello, then reveal," and your nonce is still the last input. What changes is that the attacker, when caught, has learned a random number instead of your key.

**Two freezes instead of one.** The earlier rule said a participant's "view of the session" froze at its first reveal — membership, `n`, terms, all at once. That can't work when the terms arrive *after* the reveal. So there are now two: *membership* freezes when you send your reveal (the two keys and their commitments are fixed; any further valid hello aborts), and *terms* freeze when you accept the one SESSION_INIT or PSBT_REQUEST you'll accept, which can only happen after your own user has confirmed the string. They are separate because two people confirm at different moments — one of you will have said "matches" while the other is still reading — and the spec gives separate sending rules ("not until my user has confirmed") and receiving rules so neither side can act early. The receiving rule had to be widened the same day it was written: the faster user doesn't send just one message. An initiator who has confirmed and consented sends the terms *and* her key before the other person has looked up from the SAS screen, so the slower side has to authenticate and hold both, in order, and process them after saying "matches" — or two honest people abort because one of them clicks faster. The reviewer caught that in the consensus check; the first text held only the opening message, and made even that optional.

The descriptor hash had to come out of the comparison code for the same reason the commitment went in: anything the attacker can influence *after* the honest nonces are revealed reopens the search, and an interposed attacker delivers its substitute keys after it has seen the nonces. Keeping it would have meant committing to your own key in the hello, before you have seen the terms that tell you which key to contribute, which is the flow the consent step was designed to avoid. And it is not needed: once the comparison code has authenticated the pairwise keys, every key contribution travels under those keys with authenticated sender and recipient fields, so an attacker who is not in the middle cannot alter one and an attacker who is has already been caught — now, before any contribution exists; DESCRIPTOR_CONFIRM then catches an honest divergence or a cosigner sending different keys to different peers. One rule for implementers: never reveal your nonce until you hold the other hello, and treat membership as fixed from that moment. Letting a later hello replace an earlier one, or reusing a nonce after an abort, hands the attacker a nonce before one of its own values is fixed and turns the one-in-2^59 chance back into an offline search. And keep your own nonce until you've computed and confirmed the string — the first text said to erase it "after revealing," which would have erased one of your own inputs. All of this is adopted pending external review; the study behind it, with the alternatives and an adversarial check, is `notes/sas-commitment-study-2026-11-07.md`, and the November 18 schedule change is recorded in the changelog as a decision.

**How people actually do the check (added 2026-10-08).** Four rules, each answering a way the check fails in practice. *The channel must tell you who you're talking to* — a call with people whose voices you know, in person, or a messenger where the contact is verified; never SMS or ordinary email, where the attacker who stole the code could just as easily answer for Bob. Voice was never the point; knowing who sent it is. *Everyone expected must confirm from their own screen* — the SAS catches people seeing different sessions, not an extra participant everyone sees, and an impostor who got in first displaces someone; if that someone isn't on the call, nobody notices. That's also why the screen shows the participant count, and why each client refuses more peers than expected instead of trusting the relay to. *Take turns reading* — one person reads the first group, the next the second; everyone has to look, and no one reader decides what the others hear. Studies of Signal safety numbers and ZRTP found most people confirm without looking; turn-taking makes not-looking impossible. *Make the choice real* — two equal buttons, nothing preselected, mismatch aborts.

**Why it works:** a man in the middle can't be "in" one session; it necessarily creates *two* half-sessions (attacker↔Alice, attacker↔Bob), each with different participant keys and nonces. The strings on each side are computed from different inputs and won't match. The pattern is Signal's safety numbers and ZRTP's spoken words; the commitment is what makes it deliver the security those are credited with, because without it the attacker sees both honest keys before choosing its own and can search offline for a colliding pair.

**How the SAS's history reads, in one paragraph, so nobody has to reconstruct it from dated fragments.** Draft.1 (August) hashed the session keys and the SESSION_INIT body. October 6 swapped the body for the descriptor hash, so one check covered who, what terms, and which wallet. October 8 found the flaw that undid all of it: with nothing committed, the attacker chose its values after seeing the honest ones and could search for a collision, so the 60-bit claim was withdrawn and the spec forbade advertising it. November 9 added the commitment in the hello, moved the nonce reveal into the first encrypted message, and took the descriptor hash *out* of the string, because anything the attacker can influence after the nonces are revealed reopens the search. November 18 moved the reveal to its own early message in both session kinds, so the check now runs before any content exists. Two lessons from the way survived every version and are stated permanently in §6.8: never hash envelope *headers* into the string (they differ per recipient, so honest sessions would always mismatch), and length-prefix every variable-length input so the hash can't be ambiguous.

**Why the display format is normative:** the check is two humans comparing strings across *different implementations*. If Keylay-the-app renders base-31 groups and some wallet renders hex, users are comparing apples to oranges and will shrug at mismatches. Same alphabet, same grouping, everywhere, or the check quietly stops working. And the screen must *not* show anything about the session beyond the count and the string at this point — not "setup" or "signing," not a wallet name — because the app doesn't know yet, and showing a guess would teach users that the string is about the wallet. (This is also why the SAS derivation is on Appendix B's list for expert review — a subtle flaw in *what gets hashed* is the classic way these checks fail, and it should be checked by a cryptographer, not just by us.)

**Why mandatory?** It's the detector for the one attack the code-based model can't prevent (§5.6): a code intercepted before the session starts. Prevention has a gap; detection covers it; a skippable detector covers nothing. And since November 18 it is also the gate: no ceremony message can be sent until the detector has run, so there is no way to build an implementation that skips it and still interoperates.

### §6.9 Errors and aborts

**Why so spare?** Error channels leak. Rich error responses to unauthenticated senders become an oracle (see: silent MAC failures, §5.3). So ERROR exists only *inside* the encrypted session, never changes state by itself, and ABORT is unilateral — any participant can kill a ceremony, no permission needed. In a security ceremony, the person who smells smoke must be able to pull the alarm without a quorum. And after ABORT the code burns (single-use rule): a session that died suspiciously must not be resumable by whoever made it die.

### §6.10 The Blockchain Commons relationship

**The research brief's verdict A said: adopt Request/Response, don't reinvent it. The spec... half complies. Here's the honest reasoning.**

Full adoption would mean MSP messages *are* Gordian Envelopes sealed with GSTP (their encrypted request/response protocol). The blocker is the one your own research brief flagged as "the Slide 8 ask": the Envelope/GSTP stack is Rust- and Swift-first, with **no browser-native implementation**. dCBOR plus envelope crypto reimplemented by hand in JavaScript, by a solo maintainer, as a *prerequisite* for v0.1 — that's the spec making itself unimplementable by its own reference implementation. Your June feasibility doc's core warning applies: don't let the framing force a dependency you can't carry.

So the spec takes the middle path with a straight face: adopt the *semantics* (ARID-style pairing via `mid`/`re`, the human `note` field, function-shape alignment mapped in a table), keep the *wire format* implementable in a browser today, and leave a defined slot for carrying real GSTP-sealed envelopes as opaque payloads later. That last part matters more than it looks: GSTP sealing is end-to-end across hops the session doesn't control (a QR photographed off a screen, a file at rest), which is protection MSP's hop-level channel genuinely doesn't provide. The research brief called GSTP-over-Keylay "redundant-but-correct" — the compatibility profile is where that correctness can eventually live, once the browser question has an answer. Asking the Gordian community that question is better strategy than pretending either full adoption or full independence is free.

## §7 — Layer 3: transports

### §7.1 WebSocket relay

Mostly a codification of the running app plus its security review: the 256 KB cap, rate limits, 15/60-minute timers, truncated-IP logging — all shipped and reviewed. Three things were pinned on 2026-11-18 after a reviewer found that two honest implementations could disagree on them. *The unit is a WebSocket message, not a frame:* WebSocket lets an endpoint or a proxy split one message into several frames, so "one envelope per frame" would break the moment anything fragmented; the spec now says one item per complete message after reassembly, with the size cap applied there. *The join is exact bytes:* a text message of the 64 hex characters of the channel ID, answered by `joined`, `full`, or `invalid`, so two implementations can't each invent a JSON shape. *The relay replays hellos:* Alice joins an empty room and sends her hello; Bob arrives later; a relay that only forwards to currently connected peers would satisfy the old text and Bob would never see Alice. The relay now keeps each member's hello while that member is connected and hands the set to a newcomer — safe because hellos are MAC'd and a repeated identical hello is a no-op. Two things are principled rather than inherited. **Relay non-obligations:** the relay explicitly does *not* promise ordering or delivery, because any security property you let the transport provide is a property some transport will fail to provide; everything that matters lives in the envelope. That discipline is what makes the other two bindings possible at all. **Self-hosting as a MUST:** a protocol whose only relay is yours is a service with documentation. One config field keeps that sentence false.

### §7.2 Nostr

**The single most important prohibition in the spec: no NIP-44.** This comes straight from the research brief, and it's the kind of finding that justifies the whole research effort. Nostr's standard encryption (NIP-44) derives its conversation key from the two parties' *permanent identity keys* — the same key for every message, forever, in both directions, and the NIP itself states plainly it provides no forward secrecy. Building MSP's Nostr transport on it would *silently* replace your ephemeral-key model with something strictly weaker, while looking like the responsible choice ("we used the standard"). The spec's structure — MSP envelopes unchanged *inside* Nostr events, NIP-59 gift wrap outside for metadata, throwaway Nostr keys throughout — is the brief's "rent the relay network, don't adopt its crypto," made normative.

**Why the ratchet is a precondition for shipping this binding:** Nostr relays *store* events; that's their nature and, for async, their point. But stored ciphertext changes the forward-secrecy math: session keys living on your device for the session's whole lifetime can decrypt everything a relay retained, so a mid-session device compromise now exposes *history*, not just the live moment. The May doc saw this and prescribed the in-session ratchet (Appendix A.4); the spec turns "should exist" into a shipping gate, because your public comparison page already promises FS over Nostr and a promise with a known gap needs a lock, not a note.

### §7.3 The air-gap bridge

**Why the payload's own signature carries the trust (recap from §6.6, now from the bridge's viewpoint):** the bridging participant is a scanner and typist, not a guarantor. It attests to faithful transcription (`bridged: true` keeps this visible), and the signing device's own screen — not anything the bridge carried — is what proves the key wasn't swapped in transit (§6.6a, "Verify on the signing device"). The SAS and first-address comparison backstop the rest. The alternative — coordinator signs on behalf of the air-gapped device — would make the bridge a trusted party, which is a new trust assumption smuggled in through the side door. Rejected for that reason.

### §7.4 Metadata disclosure

Every transport leaks *traffic* even when content is sealed: who's talking (IPs), when, how much, how many participants. A relay operator — yours, or a Nostr relay, or anyone tapping the wire — sees the shape of a ceremony even when every byte is opaque, and shapes correlate: an adversary matching session timing against on-chain activity can deanonymize a quorum. The spec can't eliminate this (nothing short of Tor-plus-cover-traffic can); what it can do is make *stating it* a conformance requirement, per transport, in a table. Your May doc's phrasing stands: leaving this implicit misrepresents the threat model. Marketing pressure will always push toward silence on this; a MUST in the spec is the counterweight.

### §7.5 Transports considered: Lightning, OHTTP

**Why record transports you didn't pick?** Because the first question a reviewer asks about any messaging layer in bitcoin is "why not Lightning?" — and the second is "why not just use X?" for whatever X they last read about. An answer written down once, with reasons, ends both conversations and shows the design was compared rather than assumed.

**Lightning is the more interesting of the two.** Its onion messages are free, need no payment, and are routed so that no hop knows both ends — the one property the relay and Nostr can't give you, since both see IP addresses. If that were the whole story it would beat Nostr. It isn't, for three reasons that matter more than the rest. Every participant needs a Lightning node, and a browser can't be one, so it gives up "open a page and go," which is how real people will actually use this. A node's key is a permanent identity, so it undoes the protocol's promise that nothing links one session to the next. And a node usually has channels, which are on-chain, so running a multisig ceremony from one can tie the ceremony to money — the exact correlation the metadata section warns about. So: not now, and never for browser users. But for two wallets that already embed a node, it's the natural binding, and the spec says so in a way that lets someone build it later without asking permission.

**OHTTP is the short one.** It's built for one-off, stateless questions (a DNS lookup, a telemetry ping) and needs two operators who don't talk to each other. A coordination session is a conversation, not a question, and the channel id would link its messages anyway. If you want the relay not to see IP addresses, Tor already does that with nothing changed. (The full analysis is the September 22 note.)

## §8 — Lifecycle

**Why an explicit state machine?** Because "what messages are legal right now" is otherwise decided by each implementation's accidents. States make illegal messages *definably* illegal (a PSBT_REQUEST before keys exist is a protocol violation, not a curiosity) — and testable.

**Why both timers on every party?** The 15/60-minute pair comes from the app (review-verified). Enforcing on *both* client and relay means neither can extend a session the other believes is dead. Sessions that outlive attention become unattended attack surface; ceremonies should be shorter than coffee.

**Why is rejoin so conservative?** §8.3 allows exactly one cheap path — re-announcing the *same* public key on a fresh connection, which works because the derived session keys (not the deleted X25519 private key) are what communication actually needs, and an attacker replaying that announcement gains nothing it can use — and otherwise demands a fresh session. The generous alternatives all reopen the DoS from §5.3 or create ambiguity about which keys are live — and ambiguity in key state is how protocols acquire CVEs. A multisig ceremony is minutes long; "start over" is a cost the design happily pays for state that's always unambiguous. Your May doc asked "can a participant rejoin, or is the session abandoned?" — the answer is "narrowly, and when in doubt, abandoned, on purpose."

**Why one request per session, and no retransmission (2026-11-18)?** Because the alternative is a protocol inside the protocol. The draft inferred request numbers locally and allowed several requests, and a reviewer showed the holes: a receiver might never have seen the earlier request it's supposed to count, and a lost final message leaves no counter gap at all. v0.1 takes the simple profile: one requester, one request, a new session for a different payment. A gap in the counter means "something was lost — end the session as incomplete," not "ask for it again." And the states are honest about the one case that can't be resolved: a signer that sent its signature and then lost the session doesn't know whether it arrived, so the app says "signature may have been sent," never "no signature was made."

**Why does a refusing signer send ERROR rather than ABORT (§8.4)?** Because refusal is *information*, not termination: in v0.1 the requester can only abort or try again with a corrected request in a new session, but in the planned group session m-of-n means n-of-n isn't needed and the requester may continue with other signers. Descriptor-hash and address mismatches abort unconditionally: that's not one participant failing, that's the ceremony's foundation failing.

## §9 — Conformance: the adoption wedge

The two-role split is your June feasibility doc's endgame turned into spec text. That doc's recommendation: get one aligned wallet to implement *the cheapest role* as proof, and the cheapest role is signer, not initiator. So the spec makes "signer" a first-class conformance target and keeps it small — join, send and receive key records, assemble, confirm, validate, respond, display a code and a SAS string. No slot logic, no bridging. Assembly (§6.6a) is in the signer role as of the 2026-10-06 revision, and that is a deliberate cost: every signer has to hold the descriptor anyway to refuse a foreign PSBT (§6.7), and a wallet that speaks BSMS already generates Round-2 files — §6.6a only fixes the bytes. What the signer role sheds in exchange is everything it used to do *to the coordinator's output*: no proposal to check, no acknowledgement to send to a party it doesn't trust. A wallet team should be able to read §9, scope the work in an afternoon, and not flinch. Every mandatory feature added to the signer role from here raises the price of the protocol's only realistic entry point; treat that list as guarded.

## §10 — Test vectors

**Why a spec "isn't done" without them:** prose lies; bytes don't. Two implementers can read the same paragraph, nod, and produce incompatible bytes (choose different hash input orders, different encodings of "empty," different truncations). Test vectors — known inputs with exact expected outputs at every derivation step — are how implementation #2 discovers its disagreements in an afternoon instead of in a failed interop session. Listing them as a freeze-blocker (not a nice-to-have) is the spec holding itself to the standard it will ask others to meet.

## §11–§13 — Registries, security summary, and the venue question

**Registries** are just disciplined bookkeeping: every number space (types, errors, versions...) has exactly one authoritative table, so two extenders can't grab the same number. Trivial now; impossible to retrofit after two implementations disagree.

**Why defer the standardization venue (§13)?** Your May doc listed venue choice (BIP process vs. independent spec vs. IETF) as an open question; the June doc supplied the reason it should *stay* open: the adoption case dominates the technical case, and a venue chosen before any second implementer exists is theater — process without participants. The spec substitutes something it *can* do now: a public definition of **freeze** (test vectors + two independent interoperating signer implementations + 90 quiet days). This directly enforces the May doc's own warning against premature "reference implementation" claims — the label now has a checkable definition, and §14 of the spec applies it to Keylay itself: *the current app conforms to none of this yet.* Writing that sentence into your own spec is the credibility move; wallets have watched projects call moving targets "reference implementations" before.

## Appendix A — the extensions, and why they're written but not required

### A.1 Async prekeys

**The problem:** today, forward secrecy is easy because both parties are present — keys are born, used, and deleted within one sitting. Async means Bob is asleep when Alice sends. Someone has to hold a private key *across time* so Alice has something to encrypt to; FS then demands that key still get deleted after use.

**The known solution shape** (from Signal's X3DH, via your research brief): Bob pre-publishes a *prekey bundle* — spare public keys, including one-time prekeys each used for exactly one incoming session. Alice encrypts against them while Bob sleeps and deletes her ephemeral key; Bob wakes, decrypts, and deletes the one-time prekey. Both deletions happen; FS survives. MSP's version is *simpler* than real X3DH for a nice reason: X3DH needs identity keys and signatures to authenticate strangers' bundles, but MSP peers already share `K_code` — so a MAC under the code authenticates the bundle, and the attacker-posts-a-fake-bundle-first attack (the brief's "authentication is the harder half" warning) dies without any identity infrastructure.

**The genuinely hard part is boring: where does Bob's private key live between visits?** The brief laid out three options and the spec adopts its recommendation — the **exported pending-session key file** — because of one property the others lack: *the user can see the deletion.* Delete the file, and FS is achieved by an act you can witness. Browser storage (IndexedDB) can't promise erasure — browsers don't guarantee data is gone, and copies leak into swap. A passphrase-derived key is worse than nothing for FS: something you can regenerate forever is something that *cannot be deleted*, which makes FS impossible by construction, permanently. A file is also honest in a second way: it forces the public qualifier the brief said to write yourself before someone else writes it — "no persistent state" becomes "no persistent state *except a pending-session file, whose deletion is in your hands*." That's a real cost to a marketing line, paid knowingly, and bitcoin users — who already manage wallet files and backups — are the one population for whom a keep-this-file instruction is native behavior.

**Depletion, staleness, replay rules:** each closes a specific quiet attack — drain the one-time prekeys to force a weaker mode (refuse instead of downgrading), pin an old bundle whose key the attacker eventually steals (expiry), replay a superseded bundle (monotonic counter). Unglamorous, and where async designs actually break.

### A.2 PAKE

A PAKE (password-authenticated key exchange) turns a possibly-weak shared password into a full-strength key, with the property that an eavesdropper — or someone who grabs the transcript — gets *zero* ability to brute-force the password offline; each guess requires a fresh live exchange. Why it earns its place here: async leaves bundles and transcripts sitting on relays for days, giving offline attackers something to chew on, and users *will* sometimes choose weak codes. The brief's observation makes it cheap to adopt: a PAKE needs one round trip, and async never forbade round trips — it only forbade requiring both parties awake at once. A round trip that takes a day is still a round trip. CPace is the named candidate; parameters are Appendix-B homework. One thing the November review made explicit: a PAKE only delivers "online-only guessing" if *nothing else* derived from the code is visible. Today three things are — the channel ID, the hello MAC, and (in the async profile) the bundle MAC — and each is a verifier an attacker can test guesses against offline. The split-code proposal in Q3 has to replace all three, not just the hello, before the claim is true.

### A.4 The ratchet

**What it is:** instead of one session key encrypting every message, each message gets its own key, derived from a chain — use key #12, delete key #12 and the chain state behind it, step forward. An attacker who seizes the device mid-session gets the *current* chain position, which only opens messages *from here forward* — everything already sent, whose keys are deleted, stays sealed. That's forward secrecy *within* a session, which becomes necessary exactly when transports start storing ciphertext (§7.2).

**A defect fixed before anyone built it (2026-11-18).** The first text seeded both directions of a pair from the same key with the same labels. That means message #12 from Alice to Bob and message #12 from Bob to Alice would be encrypted under the *same* key — the direction byte in the nonce keeps the ciphertexts distinct, but if Alice has sent 40 messages and Bob 12, Bob's still-live chain state can regenerate the keys Alice already deleted, which defeats the whole point. The fix is one line: derive a separate root per direction, then delete the shared pair key. Written down here because it's exactly the kind of mistake that looks fine until a reviewer traces one index.

**Its honest limits, stated because overselling ratchets is endemic:** it gives no *post-compromise security* — the property that an attacker who briefly owned your device gets locked out again afterward. A symmetric chain can't provide that; the attacker just keeps stepping the chain alongside you. Recovery requires injecting fresh DH randomness (a re-handshake), which is why the spec mandates one per 1,000 messages or 24 hours. And for *resumable* async sessions, the chain state itself must persist between visits — inside the pending-session file — which makes that file the new thing worth stealing. Every FS mechanism relocates the crown jewels; the design goal is making their location visible and their lifetime short, not pretending they've vanished.

### A.5 MLS, and why "deferred" rather than "adopted" or "rejected"

The research brief's strongest challenge: a multisig quorum is a *group*, and MLS (RFC 9420, the IETF's Messaging Layer Security) is the purpose-built standard for asynchronous group secrecy — one shared group state, membership changes as first-class operations, FS *and* post-compromise security. On pure fit-to-domain, MLS wins.

It's deferred on three grounds. **Weight:** an MLS stack in a browser is the single heaviest thing considered anywhere in this design, and it attacks Keylay's most defensible property — a single file one careful person can audit. **Scale:** pairwise's n² cost, MLS's classic justification, is irrelevant at n ≤ 7. **Session shape:** v0.1 ceremonies are minutes long with fixed membership; MLS pays off for long-lived groups that evolve. The brief's own verdict line — *"prekeys if Keylay stays a single-file app; MLS/Marmot if Keylay becomes a protocol with a real client"* — is recorded in the spec with an explicit revisit trigger (long-lived quorums with membership change), so the future decision starts from a comparison, not a rediscovery. Deferral with a written trigger is a decision; silent deferral is a time bomb. This is the former.

---

**A.6, re-scoped (2026-10-06).** Draft.1's A.6 was the patch for a distributing coordinator in sessions of three or more. With symmetric assembly that attack has no surface, so A.6 keeps only what the SAS cannot provide: evidence that survives when participants are never online together. In the asynchronous profile the `wallet_sig` on DESCRIPTOR_CONFIRM becomes mandatory, signed by the contributed cosigner key, and retained for the life of the wallet — because in an async session code knowledge is the only membership credential, and a signature under the cosigner key is the one thing a code-holder cannot forge. The irreducible human step stays: a signature proves someone holds the key, not that they are the person you meant. One sentence in A.6 was false from November 9 to November 18 and is now true: "in a live session the SAS detects an interposed party before any ceremony content is exchanged." Under the November 9 order the keys had already gone out; under the November 18 order they haven't.

**A.7, the signing-device profile (added 2026-10-08; flow revised 2026-11-18).** The question that produced it: if the browser — or any online computer — bridges your hardware wallet, why should you trust that computer at all? You shouldn't have to. If the hardware wallet does the handshake itself — you type the code on the device, it makes its own ephemeral key, it encrypts its own key record and decrypts everyone else's — then the computer just carries sealed envelopes back and forth by QR, exactly the way the relay carries them over the network. It can't read anyone's xpubs, and everything you're asked to approve (the terms, the SAS, the address) appears on the device's screen, not the computer's. The costs are real: about five QR round trips instead of one (the November 18 order adds a pass for the nonce reveal, which buys a SAS shown on a screen the bridge can't alter), the code stretch may take a long time on a small chip, and devices without keyboards need a way to enter the code that doesn't involve reading it off the computer. Hardware vendors will want a frozen, reviewed spec before touching firmware, so this is post-freeze work — written down now so nothing in the design accidentally rules it out. In the meantime, the "verify on the signing device" rule in §6.6a gets most of the safety (theft is caught) without any firmware change; what only A.7 adds is privacy against the bridging computer.

## Appendix B — the freeze-blockers, or: what this draft knows it doesn't know

The list is short but each item is load-bearing; two deserve emphasis.

**Test vectors (B.1)** — covered at §10; nothing interoperates until these exist.

**Expert review of §6.8 and A.1 (B.6)** — this is the draft stating its own epistemic position: it was assembled by careful synthesis from reviewed code, standards, and your accumulated analysis — not by a cryptographer. Most of the spec is standard constructions used in standard ways (X25519+HKDF+AEAD is as beaten a path as cryptography has). The two places it *composes* primitives into something bespoke — the SAS derivation and the code-MAC'd prekey bundle — are exactly where composition errors live, and exactly what a professional reviewer should be pointed at first. A spec that knows where it's most likely wrong is safer than one that doesn't say.
